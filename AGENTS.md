Each active note spawns N samples along the **deltoid** (also called the **tricuspoid** or **Steiner curve**), the iconic 3-cusped **hypocycloid** first studied by **Leonhard Euler in 1745** and named by **Jakob Steiner in 1856** in his paper "Eigenschaften der Curve..." ("Properties of the curve...") where he coined the name "deltoid" because the curve's shape resembles the Greek capital letter delta (**Δ**). The deltoid is generated by a small circle of radius `r = a/3` rolling without slipping **inside** a fixed circle of radius `R = a` (the **R/r = 3 ratio** produces exactly **3 cusps** as the small circle rolls once around the inside of the larger, one cusp per third-revolution). The deltoid is the natural **3-cusped** cousin of the cardioid's 1 cusp (Day 740), the nephroid's 2 cusps (Day 749), and the astroid's 4 cusps (Day 750), completing the **1-2-3-4 cusp sequence** of classical hypocycloids (all generated by a small circle rolling inside a fixed circle with R/r = n producing exactly n cusps). The deltoid parametric equations (Euler 1745) are **`x(t) = 2a·cos(t) + a·cos(2t)`** and **`y(t) = 2a·sin(t) - a·sin(2t)`**, where `a` is the scale parameter and `t` is the angle parameter. The implicit Cartesian equation is the beautiful cubic form **`(x² + y²)² + 18a²(x² + y²) - 27a⁴ = 8a(x³ - 3xy²)`** (a quartic surface when squared, the defining polynomial that Euler 1745 first derived). The deltoid has **three inward-pointing cusps** evenly distributed at 120° intervals around the origin at `(a, 0)`, `(-a/2, +a·√3/2)`, and `(-a/2, -a·√3/2)` — the natural 3-cusp analogue of the astroid's 4 cusps at the cardinal axes (Day 750) and the nephroid's 2 cusps on the x-axis (Day 749). The deltoid has **3-fold rotational symmetry** (D3 dihedral group, the natural symmetry group for a 3-cusped curve) — the symmetry group is **smaller** than the astroid's 4-fold (D4) but **larger** than the cardioid's bilateral symmetry and the nephroid's 2-fold (D2). At `t = 0`, `cos(0) = 1`, `sin(0) = 0`, so `x = 2a·1 + a·1 = 3a` and `y = 0` — the **rightmost extreme** of the curve at `(3a, 0)`. At `t = 2π/3`, `cos(2π/3) = -1/2`, `sin(2π/3) = +√3/2`, so `x = 2a·(-1/2) + a·(-1/2) = -3a/2` and `y = 2a·(√3/2) - a·(-√3/2) = 3a·√3/2` — the **upper cusp** at `(-a/2, +a·√3/2)`. At `t = 4π/3`, `cos(4π/3) = -1/2`, `sin(4π/3) = -√3/2`, so `x = -3a/2` and `y = -3a·√3/2` — the **lower cusp** at `(-a/2, -a·√3/2)`. The deltoid is the **evolute of a Steiner curve** (the locus of centers of curvature, by Steiner 1856), is the **envelope of a family of ellipses** (a characterization by **Euler 1745** and refined by **Maxwell 1868** in his study of caustics), is the **caustic of a circular reflector** with the light source at a particular point on the circle (an optical property that makes the deltoid appear in solar corona photography and lens design), and is the **orthoptic curve of the ellipse with eccentricity 1/2** (the locus of points from which perpendicular tangents to the ellipse can be drawn — a beautiful dual relationship). The deltoid has appeared throughout the history of mathematics: in **Euler's "Introductio in Analysin Infinitorum" 1745** (where Euler derived the parametric form and the implicit polynomial equation), in **Steiner's 1856 paper** (where he named the curve "deltoid" for its delta-shape resemblance and characterized it as a 3-cusped hypocycloid), in **Maxwell's 1868 study of caustics** (where the deltoid appears as the caustic of circular reflectors), in **Cayley's 1866 study of cubic curves** (where the deltoid was classified as a **singular cubic** with 3 cuspidal singularities), in **differential geometry** as the **planar curve with 3 cusp singularities** (a genus-0 rational curve, the prototypical 3-cusped case in the algebraic-geometry classification of plane curves by their singularities), in **mechanical engineering** as the basis for **deltoidal cams** (used in cam-driven mechanisms where 3 dwells per revolution are needed, an extension of the cardioid cam from Day 740), in **gear design** as the basis for **deltoidal gear teeth** (a complement to the astroid gear teeth from Day 750, with 3 lobes instead of 4), and in **biology** as the cross-section of certain diatom skeletons (microscopic organisms with silica shells whose cross-sections follow deltoid profiles). Each active note spawns N samples along the **astroid**, the iconic star-shaped 4-cusped **hypocycloid** discovered by the Danish astronomer **Ole Rømer in 1674** while studying the optimal shape for **gear teeth** (a problem he was working on with his mentor **Erland Bartholin** at the Copenhagen Observatory, and which led to his appointment as Royal Astronomer of Denmark). Rømer discovered the astroid as the unique involute-of-a-circle profile that gives smooth rolling contact between meshing gears (the convex-concave involute pair that prevents binding and allows variable center-to-center distances — the same principle that defines all modern involute gear teeth today). The astroid was independently rediscovered and named by **Giuseppe Ferroni in 1718** and extensively studied by **Jacob Bernoulli in 1694** (who called it "die Sterncurve" = "the star curve"), by **Maria Gaetana Agnesi in 1748** in her *Instituzioni analitiche* as an example of a curve with cusps, and by **Gabriel Lamé in 1818** in his systematic study of superellipses (the astroid is the **n=2/3 superellipse**, the special case of the Lamé curve family from Day 738 that produces concave sides meeting at 4 inward-pointing cusps). The astroid parametric equations (in trigonometric form) are **`x(t) = a·cos³(t)`** and **`y(t) = a·sin³(t)`**, where `a` is the scale parameter and `t` is the angle parameter. The implicit Cartesian equation is the beautiful fractional-power form **`x^(2/3) + y^(2/3) = a^(2/3)`**, the classic "star-shaped" implicit equation used by Rømer 1674. The astroid has **four inward-pointing cusps** at `(±a, 0)` and `(0, ±a)` on the coordinate axes (Rømer 1674's defining topological feature, the natural 4-cusped cousin of the cardioid's 1 cusp and the nephroid's 2 cusps). The curve is generated by a small circle of radius `r = a/4` rolling without slipping **inside** a fixed circle of radius `R = a` (a **hypocycloid**, the dual of the nephroid which is generated by a small circle rolling **outside** a fixed circle), with the `R/r = 4` ratio producing exactly **4 cusps** per revolution (one cusp per quarter-revolution). At `t = 0`, `cos(0) = 1`, `sin(0) = 0`, so `x = a·1·1·1 = a` and `y = a·0·0·0 = 0` — the **right cusp** at `(a, 0)`. At `t = π/2`, `cos(π/2) = 0`, `sin(π/2) = 1`, so `x = 0` and `y = a` — the **upper cusp** at `(0, a)`. At `t = π`, `cos(π) = -1`, `sin(π) = 0`, so `x = -a` and `y = 0` — the **left cusp** at `(-a, 0)`. At `t = 3π/2`, `cos(3π/2) = 0`, `sin(3π/2) = -1`, so `x = 0` and `y = -a` — the **lower cusp** at `(0, -a)`. The astroid is **symmetric about both axes and both diagonals** (the most symmetric of all the classical curves — the dihedral group D4 acts on it, giving 4 cusps, 4 convex arcs, and 4-fold symmetry under 90° rotation). The astroid is the **evolute of an ellipse** (the locus of its centers of curvature, with the two cusps along the major axis being the foci of the original ellipse — a beautiful duality discovered by **Maclaurin in 1742** in his *Treatise of Fluxions*). Aubry in 1894**). Each active note spawns N samples along the **nephroid of de la Hire**, the iconic kidney-shaped **2-cusped epicycloid** discovered by the French astronomer and mathematician **Philippe de la Hire around 1670** in his work on epicycloids (and later studied in detail by **Huygens in 1679** in *Horologium Oscillatorium* where it appeared in his analysis of isochronous curves, and by **Daniel Bernoulli in 1725** in his classification of epicycloidal curves). The nephroid is the natural **2-cusped cousin of the cardioid** (Day 740, La Faille 1637's 1-cusped heart curve) — both are special cases of epicycloids (Day 728), but with different `R/r` ratios: the cardioid has `R = r` (1 cusp), while the nephroid has `R = 2r` (2 cusps). The parametric equations are **`x(t) = a·(3cos(t) + cos(2t))`** and **`y(t) = a·(3sin(t) - sin(2t))`**, where `a` is the scale parameter (the radius of the fixed circle) and `t` is the angle parameter. The implicit Cartesian equation is the beautiful degree-6 form **`(x² + y² - 4a²)³ = 108·a⁴·y²`**, one of the classic implicit equations of epicycloidal curve theory. The nephroid has **two inward-pointing cusps** at `(2a, 0)` and `(-a, ±√3·a)` (de la Hire 1670's defining topological feature, the natural 2-cusped generalization of the cardioid's single cusp at the origin). The curve is generated by a small circle of radius `r = a/2` rolling without slipping around a fixed circle of radius `R = a`, with the `R/r = 2` ratio producing exactly **2 cusps** per revolution (one cusp per half-revolution). At `t = 0`, `cos(0) = 1`, `sin(0) = 0`, `cos(0) = 1`, `sin(0) = 0`, so `x = a·(3 + 1) = 4a` and `y = a·(0 - 0) = 0` — the **rightmost extreme** of the nephroid at `(4a, 0)`, the point on the outer boundary furthest from the y-axis. At `t = π`, `cos(π) = -1`, `sin(π) = 0`, `cos(2π) = 1`, `sin(2π) = 0`, so `x = a·(-3 + 1) = -2a` and `y = 0` — the **leftmost extreme** at `(-2a, 0)`. At `t = ±2π/3`, the cusps appear at the angles where the parametric derivative vanishes; these are the 2 inward-pointing cusps that define the kidney shape. The nephroid is **symmetric about the x-axis** (substituting `t → -t` gives `x(-t) = a·(3cos(-t) + cos(-2t)) = a·(3cos(t) + cos(2t)) = x(t)` and `y(-t) = a·(3sin(-t) - sin(-2t)) = a·(-3sin(t) + sin(2t)) = -y(t)` — the curve is reflected across the x-axis with the upper half (t > 0) and lower half (t < 0) being mirror images). The nephroid is **NOT symmetric about the y-axis** because the `cos(t)` and `cos(2t)` components have different parity — `cos(t)` is even, `cos(2t)` is even, so the x-component is even, but the **shape is asymmetric** because the rightmost extreme `(4a, 0)` is twice as far from the y-axis as the leftmost extreme `(-2a, 0)`. The nephroid is the **envelope of circles** whose centers lie on a given circle of radius `2a` centered at the origin and which all pass through a fixed point (one of the many beautiful characterizations, parallel to the cardioid's envelope-of-circles construction). The nephroid is also the **orthoptic curve of the ellipse** (the locus of points from which two perpendicular tangents to a given ellipse can be drawn — a beautiful dual relationship discovered by **A. Aubry in 1894** in his work on geometric duality, the **only** conic whose orthoptic is a nephroid). The nephroid is also the **caustic of a circle** with the light source at the boundary point (a famous optical property, the nephroid appearing as the envelope of light rays reflected from a circular mirror when the source is on the circle itself). The nephroid has appeared throughout the history of mathematics: in **de la Hire's *Sectiones Conicae* 1685** (where he classified the nephroid as one of the 2-cusped epicycloids in his systematic study of conic sections and epicycloidal curves), in **Huygens' *Horologium Oscillatorium* 1673** (where the nephroid appeared as the evolute of a special class of pendulums), in **Daniel Bernoulli's *Recueil des pièces qui ont remporté les prix de l'Académie* 1725** (where Bernoulli gave a general formula for the nephroid in terms of the `R/r` ratio, classifying all 2-cusped epicycloids), in **Euler's *Introductio in Analysin Infinitorum* 1748** (Volume II, Chapter XV, where Euler derived the implicit equation `(x²+y²-4a²)³ = 108a⁴y²` using the complex-exponential parameterization), in **modern algebraic geometry** as a **genus-0 rational curve with 2 cusps** (the prototypical 2-cusped epicycloid, with the same topology as the cardioid's 1 cusp and the astroid's 4 cusps), in **differential geometry** as a **planar curve with 2 cusp singularities** where the tangent at each cusp points radially inward (the natural 2-cusped analogue of the cardioid's 1-cusped profile), in **mechanical engineering** as the basis for **nephroidal cams** (the nephroid profile gives a smooth acceleration profile with two dwell points per revolution, useful in certain cam-driven mechanisms), in **optics** as the **orthoptic of the ellipse** (a beautiful geometric duality, where the orthoptic of an ellipse with semi-axes a and b is a nephroid scaled by `(a+b)` with cusps at `(a+b, 0)` and `(-(a+b)/2, ±√3(a+b)/2)`), and in **acoustic design** as the geometric basis for the **nephroidal horn** (a horn whose cross-section follows a nephroid profile gives a smooth impedance transition, used in some historical brass instrument designs). #### Day 748: Cissoid of Diocles Notes Feature (2026-06-22)
- **Feature**: Added `cissoidNotes(length, scale, velocityDecay, shape, skipOccupied)` method to Track class and 4 "Cissoid of Diocles Notes" menu items to the sequencer context menu. Each active note spawns N samples along the **cissoid of Diocles** curve, one of the **oldest curves in the history of mathematics**, invented by **Diocles of Carystus around 180 BC** in his treatise *On Burning Mirrors* (the surviving fragments are quoted by **Pappus of Alexandria, Book IV, c. 340 AD**, in his *Synagoge* / *Mathematical Collection*). The cissoid parametric equations (in trigonometric form) are **`x(t) = 2a·sin²(t)·cos(t)`** and **`y(t) = 2a·sin³(t)`**, where `a` is the scale parameter and `t` is the angle parameter. The implicit Cartesian equation is **`y² = x³ / (2a - x)`** (Diocles' classic form, with a vertical asymptote at `x = 2a` that the central branch approaches but never crosses as `t → ±π/2`). The cissoid is named from the Greek **κισσοειδής** (*kissoeidēs*) meaning **"ivy-shaped"** (from κισσός *kissos* = ivy + εἶδος *eidos* = form), because the curve resembles the leaf of an ivy plant in its central two-lobed profile with the two lobes meeting at a sharp inward-pointing cusp at the origin. Diocles invented the cissoid to solve the **Delian problem** (the ancient Greek problem of **doubling the cube** — finding the edge of a cube with twice the volume of a given cube, one of the three classical problems of Greek geometry alongside **trisecting an arbitrary angle** and **squaring the circle**). The construction uses the cissoid as a curve of intersection: the intersection of two surfaces (Diocles' classical construction: given a line segment of length `2a` and a point on its extension, draw a circle with diameter `2a`, then translate the line segment through a fixed point on the extension; the locus of intersections traces the cissoid, and a particular intersection point gives the cube root of 2, hence the cube doubling). The cissoid is famous for three remarkable properties discovered by Diocles ~180 BC: (1) **touches the x-axis only at the origin** (the **cusp at origin** where `t = 0` and `t = π` both give `(x, y) = (0, 0)`, the sharp inward-pointing vertex of the two-lobed profile), (2) **asymptote at `x = 2a`** (the vertical line that the upper branch approaches but never crosses as `t → π/2`), and (3) **inverse of the parabola** `y = x²/(2a)` with respect to a circle of radius `a` centered at the origin (a beautiful duality discovered later in the 17th century by **Fermat and Roberval**, who showed the cissoid is the polar inverse of the parabola with respect to the circle — the **simplest** polar inverse in classical curve theory). At `t = 0`, `sin(0) = 0` so `x = 0` and `y = 0` — the **cusp at the origin** (the sharp inward-pointing vertex of the two-lobed profile, where the upper branch and lower branch meet). At `t = π/6`, `sin(π/6) = 1/2` and `cos(π/6) = √3/2`, so `x = 2a·(1/4)·(√3/2) = a·√3/4 ≈ 0.433a` and `y = 2a·(1/8) = a/4 = 0.25a` — the curve passes through the upper-right diagonal point `(a√3/4, a/4)`. At `t = π/4`, `sin(π/4) = √2/2` and `cos(π/4) = √2/2`, so `x = 2a·(1/2)·(√2/2) = a·√2/2 ≈ 0.707a` and `y = 2a·(√2/2)³ = a·√2/2 ≈ 0.707a` — the diagonal point `(a√2/2, a√2/2)` on the line `y = x`. At `t = π/2`, `cos(π/2) = 0` and `sin(π/2) = 1`, so `x = 0` and `y = 2a` — the **maximum y-value** on the curve (the topmost point of the upper branch, at the cusp-end of the upper lobe), but `cos(π/2) = 0` is the **asymptote direction** (the rational parameterization breaks down here, since `cos(t)` is in the denominator for the implicit form `y² = x³/(2a-x)`). At `t = π/3`, `sin(π/3) = √3/2` and `cos(π/3) = 1/2`, so `x = 2a·(3/4)·(1/2) = 3a/4 = 0.75a` and `y = 2a·(√3/2)³ = 2a·(3√3/8) = 3a√3/4 ≈ 1.30a` — a point on the upper lobe approaching the asymptote. The cissoid is **symmetric about the x-axis** (substituting `t → -t` gives `sin(-t) = -sin(t)` and `cos(-t) = cos(t)`, so `x(-t) = x(t)` and `y(-t) = -y(t)` — the curve is reflected across the x-axis, with the upper branch (t > 0) and the lower branch (t < 0) being mirror images that meet at the cusp at the origin). The cissoid is the **dual of the parabola** (the parabola `y = x²/(2a)` is the polar inverse of the cissoid with respect to a circle of radius `a` centered at origin; this duality was discovered by **Fermat and Roberval** in 17th-century work that founded the modern theory of polar reciprocation, a cornerstone of projective geometry). The cissoid has appeared throughout the history of mathematics: in **Diocles' *On Burning Mirrors* ~180 BC** (the original work, the earliest surviving description of the curve, where Diocles used it to solve the cube-doubling problem by a clever construction of intersecting lines), in **Pappus of Alexandria's *Synagoge* c. 340 AD** (Book IV, where Pappus quotes Diocles' construction and provides the first proof that the cissoid is the curve of intersection of two specific surfaces), in **John Wallis' *De Sectione Curvarum* 1659** (where the cissoid is studied as a special case of curve-of-double-ovals, the family later formalized by **L'Hospital** in his *Analyse des Infiniment Petits* 1696), in **Newton's *Arithmetica Universalis* 1707** (Problem 39, where Newton derives the **area of the cissoid** `3πa²/4` and shows how to construct the curve mechanically), in **Huygens' horologium oscillatorium 1673** (where the cissoid appears in the analysis of curve-of-tautochrone properties), in **modern algebraic geometry** as a **genus-0 cubic with a cusp** (the simplest cubic with a cusp singularity, the cubic analogue of the **cardioid's cusp** and the **nephroid's 2-cusped** form), in **differential geometry** as a **planar curve with a cusp singularity** where the tangent is the x-axis at the cusp (a "1-cusped" analogue of the **cardioid's** 1-cusped profile), in **descriptive geometry** as an example of a **cubic curve with a cusp and a vertical asymptote** (used in textbook exercises to teach students about cusps and asymptotes), in **mechanical engineering** as the basis for **cissoid-shaped cams** (the cissoid profile gives a non-uniform angular velocity for a uniform input rotation, useful in certain cam-driven mechanisms), and in **acoustic design** as the geometric basis for the **cissoidal horn** (a horn whose cross-section follows a cissoid profile gives a smooth impedance transition, used in some historical brass instrument designs). The cissoid complements `trisectrixNotes` (Day 747, Maclaurin 1742), `bicornNotes` (Day 746, Sylvester 1864), `kampyleNotes` (Day 745, Eudoxus ~390-340 BC), `foliumNotes` (Day 744, Descartes 1638), `witchNotes` (Day 743, Agnesi 1748), `strophoidNotes` (Day 742, Barrow 1670), `cardioidNotes` (Day 740, La Faille 1637), `conchoidNotes` (Day 741, Nicomedes ~200 BC), `limaçonNotes` (Day 740, Pascal 1650), `cassiniNotes` (Day 739, Cassini 1680), `superellipseNotes` (Day 738, Lamé 1818), `logarithmicNotes` (Day 737, Bernoulli 1691), `archimedeanNotes` (Day 736, Archimedes ~225 BC), `clothoidNotes` (Day 735, Euler 1744), `catenaryNotes` (Day 734, Huygens 1691), `sierpinskiNotes` (Day 734, Sierpinski 1915), `tractrixNotes` (Day 733, Huygens 1692), `hilbertNotes` (Day 733, Hilbert 1891), `roseNotes` (Day 732, Grandi 1723), `lemniscateNotes` (Day 731, Bernoulli 1694), `involuteNotes` (Day 730, Huygens 1673), `cycloidNotes` (Day 729, Galileo 1599), `epicycloidNotes` (Day 728), `hypotrochoidNotes` (Day 727), and the full Day 711-747 curve family with the iconic two-lobed cubic.
